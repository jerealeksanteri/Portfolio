# My journey to becoming a software engineer and the lessons I learned along the way

At the time of writing this post, it is my 25th birthday. I should be happy, I should be spending my weekend without a thought about work in my head. But that's not the case here.

Before I go any further, I feel like a little intro should be included.

## The Intro *( short(ish)ly* )

I started my career as a summer worker in a _9 to 5_ office job fixing customer data for electricity retailers. I got the job through my dad. I did this through two summers. I was around 17 or 18 at that time. Then came Corona Virus and had to find something else to do. I did some gig work as an office mover and found a job categorizing and pricing screws in a fastener retailer. Fun summer, but unrelevant. 

After the summer I had half a year off school and had nothing to do. That was around the time my old boss from the office job contacted me and asked if I wanted a project. The project was simple, learn Automation Anywhere (Automation 360, RPA Framework), complete the certificate and create an automation for a customer, and get paid. I had just a tiny bit of experience in Python, but other than that I was a beginner. The No-Code environment was luckily something I had no problems with, and I finished the task easily.

I became a member of a team building Robotic Automations for legacy CIS systems. The end product was solid, but not enough to build a thriving business from it. My organization's owners and board members wanted to find solutions for these old legacy Customer Information Systems and Salesforce became our teams new target. Building a layer on top of Salesforce Service Cloud which would combine the CIS and the Billing, That was a tipping point for me. I had to learn coding. 

I spent hundreds of hours on the internet self teaching myself Python and building projects like 2D games and cli applications. The team consisted of maybe 5 people, of which none had any experience leading a software development project. You can try and guess what was the outcome. A monolith legacy-like system with a nice UI wrapper from Salesforce on top. Salaries burned through budjets and customers were unsure. And me, as a self-taught developer in my twenties, was part of it. I always knew we had something wrong, and it bothered me. Countless hours of research so I could be better for the team, thousands of hours and iterations to make the product better. I have to give a little credit to myself, I made it better. It felt exhilarating for most of the time. Some of my colleaques weren't interested in hearing out a boy's ideas, but I made them listen. I must have been irritating... 

A few years go by, years of studies in Tampere University go by, and I had taught myself from Automation 360 and Python to handling any kind of modern programming language and software development process we needed. I never backed down from an idea I had. If something was an improvement to the system it was also a goal. A lot of iterations on iterations pushing the boundaries I had myself set before. But there was always a shadow behind everything our team did. I've worked with a few colleaques who were with me the whole time through the years. People who had no real experience on software development culture before I brought it to the company. Amazing people who shared the values of self-development I had. The few always valued my propositions and I'm thankful of how I was treated by them. Nonetheless, the project always lacked a skilled, experienced team lead, and we would found ourself jumping from a development project to another time and time again when chasing demos and customer leads.

Then came the first big thing. One massive contract with a top 5 electricity retailer in Finland. I won't mention which company, but they would have an impact on, not only our team, but for the whole company. There still was a big BUT before our system could go live. It wasn't production ready. Software had flaws, but the bigger issue: system design was not there yet. This is where I got a little worried. We didn't have anyone who had designed systems before, real production-grade systems. What do I do? Hang my gloves on the wall and be done with it? No. I presented our problems to my manager and asked guidance from our company. How to build the infrastructure as code, how to design the infrastructure layer, then the application layer and so on. I was lucky enough to meet one polish guy from our company. This is where I got most of my experience as a systems developer and designer. But there was a problem. I had to focus on the infrastructure, which meant that it drew resources away from the software. Our team was short on resources and received no help from the company. This led to postponing the implementation and our customers weren't happy. We asked for more resources from the company to close the deal, but received none.

Half a year go by, the infrastructure was ready, and this polish guy I meantioned, joins the team. Exiting! The IaC was ready, made by me, but it had a few flaws here and there, that I was unsure of how to deal with. Our new team member refactored the project and I learned even more about designing the system. I refactored our release pipelines and made sure everything was ready for the implementation. Then came the blow. Our customer had resigned the contract due to unhappiness for our scheduling issues. Just as we were getting ready for the implementation. Finally our product was where it should be, but schedule and cost-wise we weren't as cheap and on-time as promised. This was huge thing for our team. This would ultimately lead to the change negotiations.

## The Change Negotiations

_Failure. Impostor. Unworthy._

For the first time in my career I felt like all the trust in myself was misplaced. For the first time I was uncertain about my future. For the first time I was unsure what I could have done better.

I felt I was a failure. Such a big part of the project was designed by ME. ME, who didn't have any experience or knowledge about software engineering 5 years ago, but somehow I managed to pull myself to the position where I could do it, I could finally do everything the project needed. That's why the change negotiations hit me so much. I had risen from nothing to something and I felt like a rug was pulled from under my feet. A wet cloth slapped to my face.

It was also about my self confidence. Was I an impostor, unworthy of the position I was in? Were the lessons learned along the way somehow wrong? So much of the responsibilities were placed on my shoulders. So much of the project's future was dragged through iterations by me. And they decided to just scrap it and move on.

***But that's business.*** As we say in finnish "tulos tai ulos". We weren't generating money and the half ready system in demos before wasn't gaining enough interest from the clients. It didn't matter now, even when the system was finally close to being ready. *It is just business.* 

**Does that define me?**
*No.*

**Does it disregard what I learned along the way?**
*No.*

**Could I have done things differently?**
*Well maybe, but I wasn't ready for that before.*

**Was this actually a good thing?**
*No, of course not. I'm being fired, you d#nkey!*

**Am I ready now, if I wasn't before?**
*No. I think I will never be "ready". I'm always thriving myself to be better. I'm ready when I'm in a grave.*

I think I'm now ready for the next chapter...

*- JN*
